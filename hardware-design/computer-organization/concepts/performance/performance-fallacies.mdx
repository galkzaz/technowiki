## Fallacy: Expecting the improvement of one aspect of a computer to increase overall performance by an amount proportional to the size of the improvement
🔴 The Fallacy
"If I improve one part of the computer by X%, overall performance will improve by X%."
This sounds reasonable.
But it is almost always wrong.

🧠 Why It’s Wrong
Because overall performance depends on how much that part is actually used.
This is exactly what Gene Amdahl formalized in Amdahl’s Law, discussed in
Computer Organization and Design.

🔍 Simple Example
Suppose:
- 30% of execution time is spent in memory access
- 70% is CPU computation
Now imagine:
You make memory 2× faster.
Many people would think:
"Great! The whole program is 2× faster."
No.
Let’s calculate.

📐 Apply Amdahl’s Law
Speedup=(1−f)+Sf​1​ 
Here:
- f=0.3
- S=2
Speedup=0.7+0.3/21​=0.7+0.151​=0.851​=1.18 
So overall speedup is only:
1.18× 
Even though memory got 2× faster.

🔎 Why This Happens
Because:
70% of the time was never improved.
You cannot accelerate what you didn’t change.

📊 Another Example (More Extreme)
Suppose:
- Floating point takes 10% of time
- You improve FP by 10×
What happens?
Speedup=0.9+0.1/101​=0.9+0.011​=0.911​=1.10× 
Only 10% improvement overall.
Even though that part is 10× faster!

🎯 The Core Principle
Performance improvement depends on:
- Size of improvement
- Fraction of time affected
Both matter.
Improving something huge that is rarely used gives little benefit.

🏗 Why Architects Care
When companies like:
- Intel
- AMD
- ARM Holdings
Design new CPUs, they must ask:
- What fraction of SPEC benchmarks is memory-bound?
- What fraction is branch misprediction?
- What fraction is vector computation?
Otherwise they risk spending silicon area for minimal gain.

🧩 Connection to CPU Performance Equation
 Execution Time=IC×CPI×Clock 
If you reduce CPI for only 20% of instructions:
Overall CPI does NOT drop by 20%.
It drops only proportionally to that fraction.

🟢 The Correct Way to Think
Instead of saying:
"I improved this by 2×."
You must ask:
"What percentage of total time does this affect?"
Then apply Amdahl’s Law.

🔥 In One Sentence
Improving one component by X% does NOT mean total performance improves by X%.
Overall improvement is limited by how much that component contributes to execution time.


## Fallacy: Computers at low utilization use little power
🔴 The Fallacy
"Computers at low utilization use little power."
This sounds logical… but it is false.
Real servers:
- Rarely run at 100% load
- Often operate between 10%–50% utilization
- Still consume a large fraction of peak power

📊 Real Observation
In Google’s warehouse-scale computers (WSCs), described by
Luiz André Barroso and
Urs Hölzle:
- Servers are at 100% utilization < 1% of the time
- Most of the time they run at 10%–50% utilization
Yet even highly tuned systems running the SPECpower benchmark from
Standard Performance Evaluation Corporation still used:
33% of peak power at only 10% workload
That is very far from proportional.

🧠 Why This Happens
Because server power has two components:
Power=Static+Dynamic 
🔹 Static Power
- Leakage current
- DRAM refresh
- Power supply losses
- Always consumed when machine is ON
🔹 Dynamic Power
- Switching activity
- Proportional to utilization
At low utilization:
- Dynamic power drops
- Static power remains
So total power does NOT drop proportionally.

📉 Example
Imagine:
Peak power = 300 W
At 10% utilization, ideal proportional system would use:
30W 
But real system may use:
100W 
That’s 33% of peak, not 10%.
That’s the core problem.

⚡ Why This Matters So Much
Datacenters:
- Contain millions of servers
- Consume massive electricity
- Pay enormous cooling costs
- Contribute to CO₂ emissions
If servers are idle most of the time but still consume 30–50% power, that’s a huge waste.

💡 The Proposed Solution: Energy-Proportional Computing
Proposed by Barroso & Hölzle:
Power consumption should be proportional to workload.
If utilization = 10%
Power ≈ 10% of peak
If utilization = 50%
Power ≈ 50% of peak
That’s the goal.

🏗 How Architects Try to Achieve This
- DVFS (Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling)
Lower frequency and voltage when load drops.
- Power Gating
Turn off unused cores completely.
3️⃣ Clock Gating
Disable inactive circuits.
4️⃣ Core Parking
Disable entire CPU cores.
5️⃣ Memory Power Scaling
Put DRAM ranks into low-power states.

🔎 Why This Is Hard
Some components are difficult to scale:
- DRAM power doesn’t scale well
- Network switches must stay active
- Power supplies are inefficient at low load
- Leakage current increases with smaller transistors

🏢 Warehouse-Scale Insight
In warehouse-scale computers:
- Thousands of machines
- Load balancing spreads work
- But most machines stay partially idle
This is why modern hyperscalers:
- Consolidate workloads
- Turn off entire servers
- Use container scheduling
- Optimize for power efficiency

📈 Important Performance-Energy Concept
Earlier we focused on:
 Execution Time=IC×CPI×Clock 
Now energy becomes:
Energy=Power×Time 
So reducing time is not enough.
If power remains high at low utilization:
👉 Energy waste continues.

🎯 Deep Architectural Lesson
In modern computing:
Performance alone is not enough.
Architects must optimize:
- Performance
- Energy
- Power proportionality
- Thermal behavior
This shift happened strongly after 2005 when power became the primary design constraint.

🟢 Clean Summary
The fallacy says:
"Low utilization means low power."
Reality:
Servers often use 30–50% of peak power even at 10% load.
The solution:
Design energy-proportional systems.
Goal:
Power∝Utilization

## Fallacy: Designing for performance and designing for energy efficiency are unrelated goals
Since energy is power over time, it is often the case that hardware or software
optimizations that take less time save energy overall even if the optimization takes
a bit more energy when it is used. One reason is that all the rest of the computer is
consuming energy while the program is running, so even if the optimized portion
uses a little more energy, the reduced time can save the energy of the whole system

🔴 The Fallacy
"Designing for performance and designing for energy efficiency are unrelated goals."
People often think:
- Performance → make it faster
- Energy efficiency → reduce power
So they assume they are separate.
But they are deeply connected.

🔑 The Key Equation
Energy=Power×Time 
This is the entire story.
Energy depends on:
- How much power you use
- How long you run

🧠 The Critical Insight
If you make something run faster, you reduce time.
Even if power increases slightly…
Total energy can still decrease.

📊 Example
Suppose a program:
- Uses 100 watts
- Runs for 10 seconds
 joulesEnergy=100×10=1000 joules 

Now you optimize it.
New version:
- Uses 120 watts
- Runs for 6 seconds
 joulesEnergy=120×6=720 joules 
Power increased.
But energy decreased.
Because time decreased significantly.

🏗 Why This Happens in Real Systems
When your program runs:
- CPU consumes power
- DRAM consumes power
- Cache consumes power
- Disk consumes power
- Network consumes power
- Cooling consumes power
All of this continues while time passes.
So if you reduce runtime:
You save energy from the entire system, not just the optimized part.

⚡ Performance Optimization Can Save Energy
Examples:
- Better Algorithm
Reduces instruction count → finishes sooner → less total energy.
- Better Branch Prediction
Reduces mispredictions → less wasted work → less time → less energy.
3️⃣ Vectorization (SIMD)
Processes more data per instruction → fewer cycles → less total runtime.

🔎 When Performance and Energy Align
They align when:
  Time reduction>Power increase 
In many cases, reducing execution time reduces energy.
This is why performance tuning is often energy tuning.

🧮 Connect to CPU Performance Equation
 Execution Time=IC×CPI×Clock 
Energy depends on:
- IC (instruction count)
- Activity (affects power)
- Voltage and frequency
If you reduce IC significantly:
- Time drops
- Dynamic switching drops
- Energy drops

🚫 When They Don’t Align
Sometimes increasing performance increases energy.
Example:
- Raise frequency
- Raise voltage significantly
Dynamic power in CMOS:
Power∝V2f 
So increasing voltage increases power quadratically.
Then:
Energy may increase even if time decreases.
That’s why DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling) is important.

🌍 Why This Matters Today
In:
- Datacenters
- Mobile devices
- Laptops
- Embedded systems
Energy is often more important than raw performance.
Modern CPUs from:
- Intel
- AMD
- ARM Holdings
All design for:
- Performance per watt
- Not just performance

🎯 Deep Architectural Lesson
Performance and energy are connected because:
Energy=Power×Time 
Reducing time often reduces energy — even if power rises slightly.

🟢 Clean Summary
The fallacy says:
Performance design and energy design are separate.
Reality:
Faster execution often reduces total energy because the system runs for less time.
So in many cases:
👉 Performance optimization = Energy optimization.
