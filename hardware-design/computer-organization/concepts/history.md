## The First Electronic Computers
ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Calculator)  is widely accepted to be the world’s first operational
electronic, general-purpose compute.

ENIAC was a general-purpose machine used for computing artillery-firing tables.
<details>
<summary>Artillery firing tables</summary>

Artillery firing tables are specialized, data-driven documents or digital tools used by field artillery to determine precise firing solutions (elevation, charge, drift, time of flight) for specific weapon-ammunition combinations. These tables account for standard and non-standard meteorological conditions to maximize projectile accuracy and target destruction. 

Key details regarding artillery firing tables:
- Purpose: They provide necessary corrections for factors like barrel wear, muzzle velocity, temperature, and wind, improving the likelihood of hitting targets.
- Content: They contain detailed performance data, including range, charge, elevation, flight time, and deflection.
- Development: Initial tables are created through computational models, which are later refined into provisional and final tables using real, live-fire data.
- Types: Tables exist for various weapons, ranging from mortar systems to heavy 155mm cannons.
- History: The first electronic computer, ENIAC, was built in 1946 to calculate these complex tables for the U.S. Army.
- Context: In training, "Artillery Gunnery Tables" (such as Table VI) are used to certify and qualify crews, ensuring combat readiness.

These tables are essential for ensuring that firing solutions are accurate, minimizing the number of rounds required to hit a target.

In size, ENIAC was two orders of magnitude bigger than machines built today,
yet it was more than eight orders of magnitude slower, performing 1900 additions
per second. ENIAC provided conditional jumps and was programmable, clearly
distinguishing it from earlier calculators. Programming was done manually by
plugging cables and setting switches, and data were entered on punched cards.
Programming for typical calculations required from half an hour to a whole day.
ENIAC was a general-purpose machine, limited primarily by a small amount of
storage and tedious programming.

##  EDVAC (Electronic Discrete Variable Automatic Computer)
In 1944, John von Neumann was attracted to the ENIAC project. The group
wanted to improve the way programs were entered and discussed storing
programs as numbers; von Neumann helped crystallize the ideas and wrote a
memo proposing a stored-program computer called EDVAC (Electronic Discrete
Variable Automatic Computer). 

EDVAC was the first stored-program computer to be built and used in the US.
It was designed by John von Neumann and his team at the University of
Pennsylvania. EDVAC was a general-purpose computer, capable of running a
wide range of programs. It was also the first computer to use a magnetic
core for memory, which allowed it to store more data than previous machines.

The magnetic core was a small, circular piece of ferrite material that
could be magnetized in one of two directions. This allowed EDVAC to store
more data than previous machines, which used paper tape or paper cards for
memory.

EDVAC also had a control unit that could fetch instructions from memory,
decode them, and execute them. This allowed it to perform complex
calculations much faster than previous machines.

##  Mark-I
Howard Aiken was building an electro-mechanical computer called the Mark-I at Harvard (a name that Manchester later
adopted for its machine). He followed the Mark-I with a relay machine, the Mark-II,
and a pair of vacuum tube machines, the Mark-III and Mark-IV. In contrast to earlier
machines like EDSAC, which used a single memory for instructions and data, the
Mark-III and Mark-IV had separate memories for instructions and data. The machines
were regarded as reactionary by the advocates of stored-program computers

## The Whirlwind project 
The Whirlwind project was a research project at MIT that aimed to develop a computer that could be used for real-time control of aircraft. The project was led by Jay Forrester, who had previously worked on the ENIAC and EDSAC. The Whirlwind project was one of the first to use a stored-program architecture, and it was also one of the first to use a magnetic core memory. The Whirlwind project was completed in 1958, and it was used to control the flight of the first supersonic aircraft, the X-15.

Whirlwind had 2048 16-bit words of magnetic core. Magnetic cores served as the main memory technology
for nearly 30 years.

## Xerox Alto
The computer that inspired many of the architectural and software
concepts that characterize the modern desktop machines was the Xerox Alto, Developed at
Xerox PARC in 1973. The Alto was created as an experimental prototype of a
future computer; there were several hundred Altos built, including a significant number that were donated to universities. Among the technologies incorporated
in the Alto were:
- a bit-mapped graphics display integrated with a computer (earlier graphics
displays acted as terminals, usually connected to larger computers)
- a mouse, which was invented earlier, but included on every Alto and used
extensively in the user interface
- a local area network (LAN), which became the precursor to the Ethernet
- a user interface based on Windows and featuring a WYSIWYG (what you see
is what you get) editor and interactive drawing programs

# IBM PC
In 1981, IBM released the IBM Personal Computer (PC), which was the first personal computer to use a microprocessor. The IBM PC was a huge success, and it helped to popularize the personal computer industry.  it featured an Intel 8088 processor, 16KB-256KB RAM, and MS-DOS.

The first personal computers were quite simple, with little or no graphics
capability, no pointing devices, and primitive operating systems compared to
those of today.
